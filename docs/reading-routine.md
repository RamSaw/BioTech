# How to read the textbook

Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell*, chapters 1–7. A background track that runs in
parallel with project work — **never a prerequisite gate**. Phase 1 starts before the book finishes.

## Order: not 1 → 7

Read **chapter 3 (Proteins) first**, and dip back into chapter 2 only when something in chapter 3
needs it (noncovalent interactions and the behaviour of water are the likely gaps). Chapter 1 is an
overview and can be skimmed whenever. Chapters 4–7 come after.

Why out of order: chapter 3 is what this project needs *now* — side chains, protein structure,
binding. Material used within days of reading it is retained far better than material read as
groundwork for something months away. Just-in-time, not prerequisite-first; the same logic as
"courses run in parallel, never as a gate" in `TRACK.md`, applied inside the book.

## The unit is a section, not a chapter

MBoC chapters are too large for one sitting — chapter 3 alone is dense enough to take several. Work
in **numbered sections**, one per reading block of roughly 60–90 minutes. A section that closes in
one block produces a workbook entry; a chapter spread over three blocks produces nothing until it is
finished, and the gap is where the thread breaks.

## The loop, per section

1. **Pre-question (5 min, before reading).** Look at the headings and the figures only — not the
   prose. Write three questions you expect this section to answer, and your guess at one of them.
   Reading with a question already in mind is a different activity from reading to absorb.
1. **Read it once. No highlighting, no notes.** Highlighting is the most reliable way to feel
   productive while retaining little: it is recognition, not recall. Notes come after the book is
   shut, not while it is open.
1. **Closed-book brain dump (10 min), by hand.** Shut the book. Write everything you can remember.
   It will feel uncomfortable and sparse. That discomfort is the mechanism — this step is where the
   learning actually happens, and skipping it turns the whole routine back into reading.
1. **Reopen and correct in a different colour.** Mark what was missing and what was wrong. Do not
   erase the wrong version. The two-colour page is the record of where your model of this was
   broken.
1. **Distil to the workbook — only what survived.** Five to ten lines in `docs/workbook/`, in your
   own words. If you cannot write it without looking, you have not learned it yet; say so in the
   entry rather than pasting something.
1. **Hand me three questions**, drawn from what the second colour revealed. They go in
   `quiz-bank.md`, and I ask them at the start of a later session.

Steps 1, 3 and 4 are the ones that do the work. Steps 2 and 5 are the ones that feel like work.

## Where I fit in

- **You read alone.** Me summarising a chapter would hand you my understanding, which is the thing
  that cannot be transferred. The book is for you.
- **No need to share the PDF.** Tell me which sections you covered and I will quiz from them. A
  450-page textbook does not fit usefully in a session's context, and I do not need it to ask good
  questions about chapter 3.
- **Paste a specific passage or figure when you are stuck on it.** That is the useful case — a
  paragraph you have read three times, not a chapter.
- **The book arbitrates, not me.** If my answer and the book disagree, the book wins and you tell
  me. I can be confidently wrong on biological detail, and you should be checking rather than
  trusting. Catching me is a sign the routine is working.

## Pace, honestly

Chapters 1–7 are roughly 400 pages of dense material. At two reading blocks in a travelling week and
three or four at home, that is **three to four months** alongside project work. Stating it plainly
so that being "only" at chapter 4 in February reads as on track rather than as failure.

The failure mode is not slowness. It is reading chapters 4–7 while Phase 1 sits untouched, because
the book always feels like the responsible thing to be doing.
