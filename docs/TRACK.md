### The thing I missed last time

The reported numbers (up to 0.95 precision) are measured against **synthetic negatives generated by
sampling amino acids from Swiss-Prot background frequencies**, on a **random train/test split**.
Both of those inflate the score:

1. **The decoys make the task too easy — which is not the same as making the labels wrong.** Random
   peptides really are almost all non-binders, so the *labels* on my decoys are fine, and sampling
   from Swiss-Prot frequencies means they are not obviously fake either. The problem is narrower:
   binders are **not** proteome-average in composition (B\*27:05 binders are Arg-enriched), while my
   decoys are. So a model could score well by counting amino acids without ever learning *where*
   they sit. The question I care about is positional, and composition alone may be answering it.
   Hence the decisive control: shuffled positives, which hold composition fixed and destroy only
   order.
1. **The split overstates performance for the use case I actually claim.** A random split over a
   dataset full of near-duplicates measures performance on peptides *similar to the training set*.
   That is a legitimate measurement — if that is the deployment distribution, nearest-neighbour
   matching is a valid model and the number is honest. But the stated deliverable is scanning a
   **novel protein** for epitopes, where by construction no close relative was in training. So the
   number answers a question I am not asking. The field's fix is similarity-based partitioning (look
   up Hobohm 1 clustering, and how NetMHCpan builds its partitions). Before applying it, state which
   claim is being made — leakage is only leakage relative to a claim.

So the current result is **not yet a result**. That is not a setback — it is the most interesting
thing in the project, and Phase 1 is about turning it into a defensible finding.

## 1. How I learn here (the method, not the content)

The constraint that matters: I can ask AI to do everything, and then I learn nothing. These rules
exist to stop that. They are not about doing things the hard way; they are about keeping the parts
that compound.

1. **I own the question, the evaluation, and the verdict. AI owns the implementation.** I decide
   what is being measured and what number would make me believe it, *before* any code exists. AI
   writes the code. I decide whether the output is real.
1. **Predict before running.** Before executing a cell or reading an AI-produced result, write down
   the number or shape I expect and why. One line is enough. Memory forms on the gap between
   prediction and outcome, not on reading a correct answer.
1. **One worklog entry per session, with a `Surprise:` field.** If nothing surprised me, I was
   either not predicting or not pushing. No entry means the session did not happen.
1. **Biology on demand, pinned to what I am building.** No more courses taken for completion's sake.
   When Phase 1 needs the HLA-B\*27:05 motif, I learn that motif properly — and I learn it well
   enough to draw it from memory, because I need it as ground truth.
1. **Retrieval, not re-reading.** Once a week, answer questions drawn from my own worklog with the
   worklog closed. Re-reading notes feels like learning and is not. (AI generating the quiz from my
   notes is a good use of AI.)
1. **Build the simplest model I can fully inspect, before the one I cannot.** Concretely: a position
   weight matrix before a transformer. A PWM has few enough parameters that I can read every one of
   them and say exactly why it got a given peptide wrong; a transformer's failure is opaque. The
   payoff is comparison — if the PWM scores 0.93 and the transformer 0.95, the transformer earned
   almost nothing, and without the PWM I would never have known that.

### What is worth learning in 2026, and what is not

What depreciated: syntax, API recall, writing pipelines by hand, remembering which function does
what. I should not spend a single hour on these.

What appreciated, in order:

- **Telling a real result from an artifact.** AI will cheerfully hand me 0.99 AUC produced by
  leakage, and it will sound confident. The ability to design an evaluation that *can fail*, spot
  leakage, demand a baseline, and distrust a good number is now the scarcest skill in applied ML. It
  is also exactly what a biotech interviewer is probing.
- **Problem framing.** Turning "find us a target for ankylosing spondylitis" into a well-posed task
  with a measurable objective, a validation set with a known answer, and an explicit statement of
  what the model is *not* allowed to be used for.
- **Enough domain prior to smell a wrong answer.** Not immunologist-level. Specifically: enough to
  look at an attribution map and know that P2-Arg should be lit up, so that when it is not, I
  notice.
- **Scale and privacy engineering** — the existing moat. This does not need building, it needs
  translating into biological vocabulary.
- **Explaining uncertainty to scientists** who will spend real money on wet-lab validation of my
  ranked list.

So the goal is not "become a computational biologist". It is: **be the person who can specify a
biological ML task, judge whether the answer is real, and build the infrastructure it runs on.**

## 2. Phases

Each phase has a **gate**: something *I* can do or state, not an artifact that AI produced. Do not
start the next phase before the gate.

### Cadence

Time available is uneven: a full weekend day when home in Munich, 3–5 hours when travelling. So the
unit of planning is **one session, not one week**, and every numbered step below is sized to finish
in a single session.

The reason is specific rather than aesthetic. A step that spans sessions has to be *reloaded*, and
after a travel gap that reload costs more than the work. A step that closes in one sitting ends with
a worklog entry, which means the next session starts from reading one page instead of from
reconstructing a mental state. That is the same failure that cost a few months last time, and it is
the one structural thing worth defending.

Two session sizes:

- **Long session (full day, home):** one numbered step that involves training or a new concept.
- **Short session (3–5 h, travelling):** one numbered step that is analysis, writing, reading, or
  packaging. Never start a training run on a short session; it will not close.

Phase totals below are in sessions, not weeks. At roughly three long plus two short sessions a
month, Phases 1–3 land around late January and Alpha runs February–March — but track progress in
sessions completed, not in dates, or the plan will feel like failure every time a trip happens.

### Phase 1 — Make Project Gamma defensible (~5 sessions)

Higher value than starting a new project. A project with an honest, hard-won evaluation beats three
shiny demos, and it is the only version of this project that survives an interview.

1. **PWM baseline.** Build a position weight matrix for HLA-B\*27:05 from positives only. Score test
   peptides with it. This teaches the motif from the inside and gives the reference every later
   result is compared against.
1. **Fix the split.** De-duplicate, then partition by sequence similarity instead of randomly.
   Re-measure all five existing setups. Expect the numbers to drop — record by how much.
1. **Hard negatives.** Build a second evaluation set from *shuffled positives*: same length, same
   amino-acid composition, motif destroyed. This is the decisive experiment. If performance
   collapses here, the model learned composition rather than binding, and the original 0.95 meant
   nothing.
1. **External reference.** Compare against a published predictor on the same held-out set
   (`mhcflurry` is pip-installable and open source; NetMHCpan is the field standard but licensed).
   Knowing the gap to the state of the art is part of the result.
1. Report **AUPRC and ROC-AUC**, not accuracy/precision/recall at an arbitrary 0.5 threshold.
1. **Consider regression instead of classification.** Binding is a spectrum, and thresholding at 500
   nM throws away the distinction between 300 nM and 480 nM while manufacturing a hard boundary
   between 499 and 501. Predicting log-affinity directly keeps that information and makes the
   threshold a decision made at the end, by whoever is using the output, rather than one baked into
   the labels. Try it as a sixth setup and compare — note that the comparison itself needs thought,
   since a regressor and a classifier are not scored the same way.

**Gate:** I can state, in two sentences and without notes, what my model is good enough for and what
it is not, and I can draw the HLA-B\*27:05 motif and explain why P2 is Arg in terms of the binding
groove.

### Phase 2 — Explainability as validation, not decoration (~3 sessions)

The standard version of this step produces a pretty attention heatmap and proves nothing. The
version worth doing treats the known motif as ground truth and asks whether attribution recovers it.

1. Integrated Gradients on the best model from Phase 1; attention maps as a secondary view.
1. **Quantify the agreement** between per-position attribution and the PWM's per-position
   information content. A correlation is a result; a heatmap is a picture.
1. Compare the frozen-head model against the fully fine-tuned one — does fine-tuning actually
   sharpen attribution onto the anchors, as predicted?

**Gate:** a figure plus one number that answers "did the model learn the biology or an artifact",
and I can defend the number.

### Phase 4 — Project Alpha: federated + DP GWAS (~8 sessions, runs alongside interviewing)

Gamma is the confidence builder and the biology on-ramp. **Alpha is the differentiator** — it is the
only project that sits directly on top of five years of distributed-systems and differential-privacy
work, and it is what makes Owkin / Tune Insight / Verily-type roles a conversation rather than an
application. Do not skip it to start something new.

This phase deliberately overlaps with interviewing (see §3). Alpha's spec should be written *after*
the first one or two interviews, so that what those conversations reveal about what employers
actually probe shapes what gets built. That is the whole argument for interviewing before the
portfolio is "finished".

Sketch (to be specified properly when Phase 3 closes): 1000 Genomes split into simulated hospital
nodes, federated chi-square association statistics via Flower, DP noise applied locally, and a
privacy-versus-utility curve as the headline figure. Target an HLA-region or IL23R locus so it stays
tied to the autoimmune thread.

**Gate:** the privacy/utility curve, and a crisp verbal answer to "what exactly leaks, and what does
epsilon buy you".

### Interview timing (decided 2026-11-21)

Start interviewing **when Phase 3 closes** — one or two real processes, deliberately not a campaign
— and keep building through them. The reasoning:

- Interviews are the only honest measurement of this whole effort. Everything before them is
  self-assessed, and self-assessment of a career change is unreliable in both directions.
- They are also the fastest way to find out which gaps actually matter. Three months of guessing
  what a TechBio hiring manager wants is worth less than one hour of being asked.
- One or two, not ten: enough signal, while a rejection costs little and the good targets stay
  unspent until Alpha is in hand.

Practical consequence: **networking starts now, applications start at Phase 3.** Bits in Bio Slack
and Health AI Munich this month, because warm conversations have a long lead time and cost nothing
to start, and because the people in them will tell me whether this plan is aimed correctly.

After each interview, a worklog entry: what they probed, what I could not answer, what that implies
for the next phase. Treat it as data collection, not as a verdict on me.
